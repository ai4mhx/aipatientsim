# aipatientsim

Data and analysis code for the study of language model simulated psychiatric
patients described in:

> [Author list]. Structural fidelity and unreliable affective evaluation in
> language model simulations of psychiatric patients. Submitted to
> *Communications AI & Computing* (2026).

Three consultant psychiatrists, blinded to origin, independently rated 42
anonymised psychiatric interview transcripts. Thirty-six were generated by
ChatGPT-4o, Gemini 2.5 Flash and Claude Sonnet 4 across a structured matrix of
age group, sex and ICD-11 diagnosis. Six were interviews with real outpatients at
the National Institute of Mental Health and Neurosciences (NIMHANS), Bengaluru,
India, and serve as a reference distribution.

The study asks two questions. How faithfully do frontier models reproduce the
behavioural surface of psychopathology when asked to enact a psychiatric patient?
And can expert human judgement, the default instrument in this literature,
measure that faithfulness reliably?

### What is not here

The six transcripts of interviews with human participants are **not** included and
will not be released. Participants consented to verbatim transcription for
analysis but not to public release, and a psychiatric interview transcript cannot
be reliably de-identified without destroying the very features the study
measures. Their ratings are in `data/ratings_master.xlsx` under identifiers
beginning with `D`, so every analysis in the paper is reproducible from this
repository even though the underlying human text is withheld. De-identified
excerpts may be made available to qualified researchers on reasonable request to
the corresponding author, subject to approval by the NIMHANS Departmental Ethics
and Scientific Committee.

## Transcript identifiers

Every transcript has a four or five character identifier that encodes its
provenance and the persona parameters it was generated under. The scheme is the
one used throughout the paper, the rating spreadsheet and the file names in
`transcripts/`.

| Position | Meaning | Values |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Source | `A` ChatGPT-4o · `B` Gemini 2.5 Flash · `C` Claude Sonnet 4 · `D` human participant |
| 2 | Sex | `M` male · `F` female |
| 3 | ICD-11 diagnosis | `X` generalised anxiety disorder · `Y` schizophrenia |
| 4 | Age group | `1` adolescent, under 18 · `2` adult, 18 to 59 · `3` geriatric, 60 and over |
| 5 | Participant serial | human transcripts only, where two share the same cell |

`BMY3` is a geriatric male with schizophrenia generated by Gemini 2.5 Flash.
`DMX21` is the first of two adult male human participants with generalised
anxiety disorder.

### Sample composition

Each model contributed 12 transcripts, one per cell of the 2 (sex) × 2
(diagnosis) × 3 (age group) matrix, generated twice. All six human participants
were adults, three with each diagnosis.

| | GAD | Schizophrenia | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| ChatGPT-4o | 6 | 6 | 12 |
| Gemini 2.5 Flash | 6 | 6 | 12 |
| Claude Sonnet 4 | 6 | 6 | 12 |
| Human participants | 3 | 3 | 6 |
| **Total** | **21** | **21** | **42** |

### Phase allocation

The 42 transcripts split into two overlapping analyses.

**Phase 1**, human versus model-generated, uses 12 transcripts: the six human
interviews and six model-generated interviews matched to them on age group and
diagnosis, two from each model.

```
AFX2  BFX2  CMX2  AFY2  BFY2  CMY2
DFX2  DMX21 DMX22 DFY2  DMY21 DMY22
```

**Phase 2**, model versus model, uses the remaining 30 model-generated
transcripts, ten per model. The inter-rater agreement analysis is computed on
these 30.

## Generation protocol

All three models were accessed through their public consumer interfaces in
September and October 2025, in default configurations. No fine-tuning, no
developer or system-level instructions, and no custom personas beyond the
in-conversation anchor prompt. Temperature and other decoding parameters were not
exposed by these interfaces and were left at product defaults. This
characterises out-of-the-box behaviour as a practitioner would encounter it,
not the ceiling achievable with prompt engineering.

Each simulation opened with an anchor prompt specifying only age, sex and ICD-11
diagnosis. Subsequent turns were not scripted: each question was shaped by the
model's preceding response, so the persona could be probed rather than recited.
All interviews, with models and with human participants alike, were conducted by
the principal investigator, to reduce interviewer variance.

Model versions change. Results here describe the specific snapshots available in
September and October 2025 and should not be assumed to transfer.

## Rating data

`data/ratings_master.xlsx` holds 126 rows, one per transcript per rater, on the
`MASTER SHEET` tab. Raters are coded `R1`, `R2` and `R3`, each an Assistant
Professor at NIMHANS with at least three years of post-MD clinical and teaching
experience. All were blinded to transcript origin, to the model of origin, and to
the target diagnosis, and rated independently without communicating.

Ratings were collected with the Psychiatric Interview Assessment Questionnaire
(PIAQ), reproduced as administered in
`Psychiatric Interview Assessment.pdf`. The instrument was developed for this
study and has not been psychometrically validated; its reliability is itself an
object of study in the paper rather than an assumption.

### Scored domains

Item-level scores and a total are stored for each domain. Totals are the columns

### Derived coding

Two conventions used throughout the analysis are worth stating explicitly,
because results depend on them.

**Dichotomisation.** For the agreement analysis, each ordinal domain is
dichotomised at its scale maximum: code 1 if the rater awarded the maximum
attainable score, code 2 otherwise. This is the coding specified on the `CODES`
tab of the workbook.

**Majority agreement.** For multi-select qualitative items (F1, F2, K3), a
response counts as endorsed when two or more of the three raters selected it.

## Reproducing the analyses

```bash
pip install -r code/requirements.txt
python code/analysis.py      # Phase 1, Phase 2, diagnostic accuracy
python code/agreement.py     # AC1, Fleiss kappa, raw agreement, per-domain
python code/figures.py       # Figures 1 to 5
```

Results in the paper were produced in Stata 17 (StataCorp, College Station, TX).
The Python scripts here are an independent reimplementation, provided so that
every number can be checked without a Stata licence. Where the two differ, the
difference is documented below.

### Statistical procedures

Domain scores are ordinal and not normally distributed, so all comparisons use
non-parametric tests and are summarised as medians with interquartile ranges.

- **Phase 1**, human versus model-generated, n = 6 per group: Mann-Whitney U with
  exact p values. Effect size is the rank-biserial correlation, positive where
  model-generated transcripts scored higher, with a bootstrap 95% confidence
  interval over 6,000 resamples.
- **Phase 2**, three models, n = 10 per model: Kruskal-Wallis with pairwise
  Mann-Whitney U where the omnibus test is significant. Effect size is
  epsilon-squared with a bootstrap 95% confidence interval.
- **Categorical outcomes**: Fisher's exact test.
- **Agreement**, across all three raters on the 30 Phase 2 transcripts: Gwet's
  first-order agreement coefficient, AC1.

All tests are two-tailed with alpha 0.05.

### Why AC1 rather than kappa

This is the single most important methodological choice in the repository, and it
is easy to get wrong when re-running the analysis.

Several domains are heavily skewed toward the scale maximum: diagnostic clarity,
for instance, is scored at maximum on 89% of transcripts. In that regime expected
agreement by chance approaches observed agreement, so chance-corrected kappa
divides a small numerator by a small denominator and collapses toward zero even
when raters agree almost perfectly. This is the first kappa paradox (Feinstein &
Cicchetti, 1990). Gwet's AC1 (2008) replaces the kappa chance-agreement term with
one that skewed marginals do not inflate.

The contrast is stark on these data and is reported in the paper as a finding in
its own right. On the identical dichotomised codes, Fleiss' kappa is at or below
zero for **every** domain, including those where AC1 exceeds 0.90, while raw
pairwise agreement tracks AC1. `code/agreement.py` prints all three side by side.

| Domain | AC1 | Fleiss kappa | Raw agreement |
|---|---|---|---|
| Clinical usability: training | 0.929 | −0.034 | 0.93 |
| Diagnostic clarity | 0.876 | −0.059 | 0.89 |
| Clinical judgement | 0.818 | −0.084 | 0.84 |
| Symptom presentation | 0.569 | −0.120 | 0.69 |
| Safety exploration | 0.477 | −0.111 | 0.64 |
| Emotional expression | 0.111 | 0.018 | 0.53 |
| Psychological realism | 0.034 | 0.010 | 0.51 |
| Clinical credibility | −0.153 | −0.158 | 0.42 |

If you recompute agreement with Cohen's or Fleiss' kappa and find that
everything is near zero, that is the expected behaviour of the statistic at this
degree of marginal skew, not an error in the data.

Note also that Cohen's kappa is defined for two raters only. With three raters,
use Fleiss' kappa or report the three pairwise Cohen coefficients; `agreement.py`
does the latter as well.

### Known data issue, resolved

In an earlier version of the rating workbook, rater R2 had two rows labelled
`AMX1`, submitted ten minutes apart, and no row for `AMX2`. The second was in
fact `AMX2`, mislabelled at data entry, and has been corrected in the file
published here. All three raters now have exactly 42 distinct transcripts. Any
analysis run against the earlier file will differ slightly for ChatGPT-4o
comparisons under R2.
